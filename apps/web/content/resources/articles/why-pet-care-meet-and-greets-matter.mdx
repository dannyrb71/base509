Trust should begin before a leash changes hands.

A meet and greet gives a pet owner and a prospective care provider time to see
how the pet responds, talk through the care routine, and decide whether the
relationship feels right. That matters for every first booking—not only when a
dog may be around other animals or children.

Pet Sitters International recommends an in-person meeting because it lets an
owner see a provider interact with their pet and gives both people time to
review services, credentials, and policies. The meeting cannot predict every
behavior, but it can surface questions that a form or text exchange may miss.

## Chemistry is part of the care plan

Dogs do not respond to every person in the same way. A dog may be social with
one new person, cautious with another, or too excited to settle. Watch the
interaction without forcing it. Is the dog able to approach at their own pace?
Do they relax after the initial greeting? How do they respond when the provider
moves around the room, reaches for a leash, or follows the owner's normal
handling routine?

A dog may also behave differently after the owner leaves. Some become more
reserved or vocal; others settle and form a temporary attachment to the person
caring for them. The goal is not to promise an instant bond. It is to identify
what helps the pet feel secure and to make a plan for the transition.

If the pet will encounter children or other animals during care, discuss that
environment explicitly. Do not treat a friendly first greeting as permission
for unsupervised contact. The owner should disclose known history and triggers,
and the provider should explain how introductions, separation, and supervision
will work.

<Callout title="A meet and greet is a fit check, not a sales formality">
  Either person should be able to decline the booking if the environment,
  service, or chemistry is not right. A respectful “not this time” is safer than
  hoping a poor match will work itself out after the owner leaves.
</Callout>

## Ask about behavior in real situations

“Is your dog friendly?” rarely produces enough useful information. Ask how the
pet behaves in specific situations:

- Where does the pet normally sleep, rest, eat, and go to feel safe?
- How do they respond to unfamiliar adults, children, dogs, cats, bicycles,
  delivery workers, or visitors at the door?
- What happens on leash when another dog or person approaches?
- Do they guard food, toys, furniture, doorways, or a favorite person?
- Are they frightened by storms, fireworks, alarms, crates, touch, or being
  left alone?
- Have they ever escaped, bitten, snapped, or injured another animal?
- What signs show that they are anxious, overstimulated, in pain, or ready for
  space?
- Which cues and rewards does the owner use consistently?

Pet owners should answer plainly, even when a behavior feels embarrassing.
Providers need accurate information to decide whether they have the right
environment and experience. Providers should be equally candid about the
number and types of animals present, access to children, containment, and how
much direct supervision the service includes.

## Walk through food, allergies, and the daily routine

Ask what the pet eats, how much, when, and where. Confirm which treats and
chews are allowed, how food is stored, and whether pets in a shared environment
must eat separately.

Document known food sensitivities and allergies. Chicken is among the proteins
that can cause food allergies in some dogs, but neither an owner nor a provider
should diagnose an allergy during a meet and greet. A veterinarian can help
evaluate symptoms and determine an appropriate diet.

The same caution applies to an upset stomach. Do not assume that chicken, rice,
pumpkin, fasting, or another home remedy is appropriate for every dog. Record
the owner's veterinarian-approved plan, including what the provider may give,
how much, when to call the owner, and when veterinary care is required.

The [American Animal Hospital Association's pet-sitter
guidance](https://www.aaha.org/resources/preparing-for-the-unexpected-essential-pet-sitter-instructions/)
also recommends documenting the pet's normal schedule, activity, restrictions,
favorite hiding places, and the location of supplies. Familiar routines can
help reduce stress when the owner is away.

## Review every medication and supplement

Create a complete list of prescriptions, over-the-counter products, and
supplements. For each one, record:

- the exact name and dose;
- the time and frequency;
- how it is administered;
- storage requirements;
- what to do if a dose is late, missed, refused, or vomited; and
- the veterinarian or emergency contact to call with a concern.

Whenever possible, have the owner demonstrate the normal method and let the
provider explain it back. Confirm that enough medication will be available for
the entire booking in its original labeled container. A provider should never
change a dose or substitute a product without authorization from the owner and
the prescribing veterinarian.

## Agree on emergencies and decision-making

Before care begins, both people should know what happens if the owner cannot be
reached. Confirm:

- the primary veterinarian and nearest emergency clinic;
- a local backup contact who knows the pet;
- relevant medical conditions and warning signs;
- transportation expectations;
- authorization for urgent veterinary care; and
- how expenses and reimbursements will be handled.

For in-home care, also review access instructions, alarms, locks, cameras, and
areas that are off-limits. For care in the provider's home, review gates,
fencing, sleeping arrangements, separation areas, and the household's emergency
plan.

## Set arrival rules before the first handoff

Clear arrival expectations protect the provider's time and help keep the dogs
calm. If care takes place in the provider's home, it is reasonable to require a
specific estimated arrival time before every drop-off and pickup.

“On my way” may not be enough when clients are traveling from different places.
Ask for an updated arrival estimate, such as “about 20 minutes away,” and have
the provider meet the client outside when practical. That can prevent a ringing
doorbell from setting off the household or creating a difficult doorway
handoff.

Live location sharing can be helpful, but it should remain optional. An accurate
arrival estimate and a message when the client is outside are usually enough.
Put the policy in writing so it feels like a normal part of service rather than
a correction directed at one client.

## Make the first booking easier to evaluate

When practical, begin with a lower-stakes service before a long stay: a walk, a
drop-in visit, daycare, or a shorter overnight. This does not replace the meet
and greet, and it does not guarantee that every future visit will be identical.
It gives the provider more information about how the pet settles after the
owner leaves and gives the owner a chance to evaluate communication and care.

Afterward, share what the pet ate, how they rested, bathroom habits, social
interactions, medication administration, and anything that differed from the
care plan. Specific updates build more confidence than a generic “everything
was great.”

## Good service is personal—and sustainable

Pet care is a service relationship. Owners are trusting someone with a family
member; providers are responsible for being dependable, prepared, and as
flexible as safety and capacity allow. Clear boundaries and a service mindset
belong together.

Always thank the client. Learn the pet's preferences and remember the human
details clients choose to share. An occasional complimentary add-on, small
credit, or thoughtful gesture can recognize a loyal relationship without
turning generosity into an automatic entitlement.

There may also be moments when compassion matters more than a standard policy.
A provider may choose to reduce or waive a fee when a longtime client is
traveling for a family emergency or loss. That decision should be voluntary,
financially sustainable, and documented consistently. The value is not the
discount alone; it is showing the client that they and their pet are known.

## A meet-and-greet checklist to keep

Before confirming the first booking, make sure both people can answer these
questions:

1. Does the pet appear reasonably comfortable with the provider?
2. Have behavior, triggers, bite or escape history, and social boundaries been
   discussed honestly?
3. Is the feeding plan specific, including allergies and forbidden foods?
4. Are medications, supplements, and administration instructions complete?
5. Are veterinarian, emergency, and backup contacts current?
6. Does everyone understand the service, supervision, home environment, and
   arrival rules?
7. Is the owner comfortable with the provider's policies—and is the provider
   confident they can meet the pet's needs?
8. Are the final instructions written somewhere both people can access?

The best meet and greets feel less like an interview and more like the beginning
of a working relationship: honest, attentive, and centered on the pet. Taking
that time before the first booking gives the owner, provider, and animal a
better chance to begin with trust.
